# Offers lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **A dream outcome buyers already understand.** "Know a layout change will work before you move the shelf" maps directly to a goal a store manager states in money terms (e.g. "+10% snack sales, ₱30k budget"). Each experiment ends in a sales metric, not a dashboard.
- **Less effort and sacrifice for the buyer.** The tool is hardware-agnostic and works from existing CCTV and POS exports, so the buyer installs no new sensors. The incumbent the founder names, RetailNext, builds its analytics around its own proprietary sensor. That difference is a real way to lower effort, not just a lower price.
- **Not positioned as the cheaper option.** The founder frames StoreLab as adding something new (AI-designed, pre-simulated experiments), not as a cheaper RetailNext. That is the right instinct.
- **The pieces of a stack exist already.** Experiment design, constraint checking, simulation and a pilot plan with a power calculation solve the buyer's problems one by one ("what should I try?", "will it break the queue?", "how do I test it properly?"). All of them are software, so each extra piece costs little to deliver.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Perceived likelihood is close to zero today.** There are no customers, no pilots and no real store data. The only validation is synthetic data drawn from the same model family. A buyer is being asked to trust a simulator that has never ranked a real-world winner. Most offers die from missing proof, and this one has none yet.
2. **The time to a result is long and unclear.** The value only becomes real after a physical pilot of test stores against control stores, sized by a power calculation. The buyer pays before learning anything. Nobody yet knows how many pilots a buyer needs before trusting the tool (the founder lists this as open).
3. **The market's pain and money are unproven.** We do not know if mid-size chains feel "bad layout guesses" as a massive pain or a minor one, or if they have budget for analytics software. The market could be weak even with a good offer. Open questions: willingness to pay goes to /founder-consumer and /founder-pricing; segment size and spend go to /founder-competitors and /founder-cfo.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Which single buyer (job title) owns layout decisions and a budget? What does one bad layout change cost them today?
- Will you guarantee the first pilot? For example: if the recommended change does not beat control on the agreed metric, the buyer does not pay the pilot fee.
- How fast can a new chain get its first simulated recommendation from real CCTV and POS data: days or months? Is your existing CCTV assumption true for their cameras?
- Could you charge double and still be the obvious choice? What would have to be in the stack for that?
- Which of your two pricing ideas (per store or per pilot) puts the payment after the proof?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Get proof before scaling.** Run at least one real-store pilot with a design partner where the simulator's ranking is checked against real results. Show that result, win or lose.
2. **A guarantee-led first offer.** Lead with a per-pilot offer that has an outcome or refund guarantee, not with an open-ended per-store subscription. Turn on the subscription only after the simulator has earned trust. Exact price points stay open (/founder-pricing, /founder-cfo).
3. **A named, stacked offer.** Package a fixed-scope "first experiment": a CCTV readiness check, a goal-to-recommendation run, a constraint-checked layout, a pilot plan, and a results readout. Give it a clear name and a firm timeline. Only add scarcity that is real, such as a limited number of design-partner slots.
4. **Evidence that the market has money.** Confirm that the chosen segment has layout or merchandising budget and feels the pain (/founder-consumer).

## Recommended first customer segment
**(a) Mid-size Philippine convenience/mini-mart chains (20–500 stores, existing CCTV).**
- **Easiest to find and reach.** It is one country and a known category, and the founder can pitch in Filipino and English. It is the narrowest of the three options to target.
- **Fits the proof the founder already has.** The MVP was built on a convenience store. Small, repeated store formats across many locations make test-vs-control pilots feasible and lower the effort per experiment.
- **Lowest effort for the buyer.** The segment is defined by already having CCTV, which matches the no-new-hardware promise.
- **Avoids the strongest incumbent where it is strongest.** JAPAC grocery and mall retailers are larger buyers who are more likely to have existing analytics vendors, possibly including RetailNext with its Tokyo, Shanghai and Singapore offices. That is unverified (/founder-competitors).
- **The open risk.** Whether this segment has the money and feels the pain strongly enough is unproven. Validate it first (/founder-consumer, /founder-pricing).
