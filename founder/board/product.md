# Product lens
Score: 5 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **The one-sentence pitch already exists.** "Test store changes virtually before changing the real floor" is clear, and the before/after loop ("idea → change → wait → measure → undo" vs "observe → simulate → shortlist → test → learn") explains the product without a paragraph.
- **The experience starts in the right place.** A manager types a goal in plain language with a budget and a guardrail ("don't worsen the checkout queue"). That's the customer's moment, and the product works back from it.
- **Care in the parts nobody sees.** The AI never computes the numbers, hard constraints override the AI, predictions are treated as hypotheses to test, and pilot results feed back into the model. These are craft choices, and the founder labels synthetic results honestly.
- **No new hardware to install.** It uses cameras and POS exports the store already has, so the buyer has less to do.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Too many things, none of them brilliant yet.** Today it is analytics, a digital twin, AI experiment design, simulation, pilot planning and multilingual support across three segments. The founder says the observation layer is not new, and a known competitor already sells it along with pilot-vs-control testing. If the "simulate before you move the shelf" moment isn't the one excellent thing, the product reads as a weaker version of what's already on the market.
2. **Seams the product does not own.** The experience depends on things outside StoreLab's control: the store's CCTV (quality unknown, and the founder lists it as an open question), POS export formats, a manager's goal, a head-office approval, staff physically moving fixtures, and the test/control pilot. A break at any of these points ruins the outcome, and the customer will blame StoreLab.
3. **The trust gap is the product.** The MVP proves the mechanism only on synthetic data from the same model family. Until the simulator ranks a real winner above a real loser, the headline experience ("we predicted this would work") doesn't exist yet. Nobody knows how many pilots it takes to earn that trust.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- In one sentence, with no list, what is the one thing StoreLab must do brilliantly? What gets cut for the first customer (heatmaps? multilingual? the free-text goal box?)
- Walk me through a store manager's first five minutes, and their first 30 days. What do they see before StoreLab has learned anything about their store?
- Who sets up the cameras and data feeds, who approves the change, and who moves the shelf? Which of those hand-offs does StoreLab own?
- What's the minimum CCTV and POS quality the product needs, and what happens when a store falls short?
- What does the manager see when the simulator turns out to be wrong in a pilot?
- How the price is structured (per-store vs per-pilot) shapes the experience. Which one makes the first pilot easiest to say yes to? (Open: /founder-pricing, /founder-consumer.)

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Cut to one hero flow for one segment.** One goal type (e.g. category sales lift under a queue constraint) runs end to end through recommendation and pilot plan in one store format. Other goal types, languages and segments wait.
2. **Publish an onboarding spec that removes the seams:** minimum camera and POS requirements, a data-readiness check before any promise is made, and a named owner for every hand-off up to the pilot readout.
3. **Run one real pilot loop on real store data** and publish the honest result: did the simulator's ranking match reality? Report it whether it did or not.
4. **Write a one-page experience story for the first customer** (first five minutes, first pilot, first result) before adding more features.

## Recommended first customer segment
**(a) Mid-size Philippine convenience/mini-mart chains, 20–500 stores, with existing CCTV.**
- **Simplest experience to own end to end.** Convenience stores are small and their formats tend to repeat. Of the three segments, these are the fewest aisles, fixtures and zones to model, and the MVP is already built on a convenience store.
- **Test and control stores come built in.** A chain of 20–500 similar stores provides comparable sites for pilots without much bespoke work.
- **Fewer seams.** One country and one or two languages means one set of CCTV, POS and approval patterns to understand first. A mid-size chain may also have a short path from goal to approval to shelf move (to be confirmed: /founder-consumer).
- **Why not (b) or (c):** JAPAC grocery adds bigger, more varied floors, many languages and markets, and puts StoreLab in reach of an established competitor with offices in the region. Mall specialty and department stores tend to be more bespoke per store, and landlords and brands add hand-offs StoreLab doesn't control.
- **Open questions:** segment size, willingness to pay, and CCTV quality in these chains are unknown (/founder-consumer, /founder-competitors). Pilot economics and price per store or per pilot are also unknown (/founder-pricing, /founder-cfo).
